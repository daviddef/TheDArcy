## Why this is him beyond doubt

| The will says | The archive holds |
|---|---|
| Major General in the Army | Major-General, 12 August 1819 (Connolly's *Roll*, entry 203) |
| Colonel of the Royal Engineers | Colonel, 1813; C.B. |
| of Chatham, Kent | "Died at Chatham, 13 5 1827"; buried St Mary, Chatham, 19 May 1827 |
| proved 4 July 1827 | died 13 May 1827 — seven weeks, which is normal |

There are only **two D'Arcys in the entire Roll of Officers of the Corps of Royal Engineers,
1660–1898**, and the other is his son Constantine, who died in 1805. No other man can fit this
description.

## Why it matters more than anything else outstanding

**Robert D'Arcy's parentage is the single biggest open question on this site.** Seventy-one of Ian's
two hundred ancestors — and twelve of his twenty-two generations — hang on the join immediately above
this man, and that join is unevidenced. His whole adult life is on paper and **not one record of it
names a father**: not his commission of 17 January 1776, not the Corps roll, not his marriage, not
his burial. The *Gentleman's Magazine* did not notice his death.

A will of 1827 routinely names:

- **children and grandchildren**, with their married names
- **executors** — usually kin or close associates
- **legacies to relations** the record otherwise never mentions
- sometimes the testator's **own origins**, a birthplace, or an inherited property

For a man who may have been an acknowledged natural son, **the people he chose to leave money to are
the strongest indirect evidence obtainable**. And a null is nearly as useful: a will showing a man
with no relations beyond his own children would argue against any grand connection at all.

## And a null result, recorded so nobody repeats it

**The 4th Earl of Holderness's will was searched for on 9 September 2026 and does not appear to
exist in the Prerogative Court records.** Queried under *Darcy*, *D'Arcy*, *Holderness* and
*Holdernesse*, and across every PROB record dated 1778–1780. Nothing.

That is not proof he left no will — peers' estates commonly passed by marriage settlement rather than
testament, and his heir was his daughter Amelia, Baroness Conyers, so there may have been little to
devise. But the hoped-for acknowledgement of a natural son is not in the obvious place, and
`/hornby` now says so.
